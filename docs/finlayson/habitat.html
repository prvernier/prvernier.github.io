6  Habitat requirements

6.1 Seasonal range overview

The Finlayson caribou herd (FCH) is a Northern Mountain (NM) woodland caribou population (Rangifer tarandus caribou) that undertakes pronounced seasonal migrations between low-elevation winter range and high-elevation alpine/subalpine summer range. Northern Mountain caribou migrate seasonally, moving from the open boreal forest in winter up into the subalpine and alpine in summer — though some herds also spend much of the winter on windswept alpine slopes. The table below summarises the FCH’s known seasonal habitat associations, drawn from the population survey reports and the broader Northern Mountain caribou literature.

Season Elevation / terrain Key habitat features Primary forage
Winter (Dec – Mar) Low-elevation lowland forest; sheltered from prevailing SW weather by Pelly Mountains Open black spruce, white spruce, lodgepole pine forest with abundant ground lichens; low snow due to mountain rain shadow Terrestrial lichens (Cladonia/Cladina spp.) excavated by cratering through snow
Spring (Apr – May) Transitional; animals move from lowland winter range upward; females typically lead South-facing slopes and riparian areas that snow-free early; access to fresh green vegetation Early green-up: forbs, sedges, new shrub growth; supplemented by lichens
Calving / post-calving (May – Jun) High elevation subalpine/alpine; females disperse widely and are largely solitary Isolated subalpine/parkland habitat; low moose density reduces wolf presence (predator avoidance) Forbs, sedges, graminoids; diverse high-quality forage to support lactation
Summer (Jul – Sep) Upper subalpine and alpine plateaus south of Finlayson Lake; ~1/3 of herd uses alpine north of Robert Campbell Hwy Open alpine tundra, sedge meadows, insect-relief habitat (ridges, icefalls, snowfields) Forbs, graminoids, dwarf shrubs, fungi, sedges; diverse diet
Rut / fall (Oct – Nov) Alpine plateaus in Pelly Mountains; animals concentrate at higher elevations Sub-alpine rut habitat; bulls seek concentrated cow groups; overlaps Pelly Mountains mineral belt Residual forbs, shrubs; energy reserves built for rut and approaching winter

6.2 Winter habitat — the critical season

Lichen as the keystone winter resource

Northern Mountain caribou are the only animals that forage substantially on lichen in the winter. Terrestrial lichens are the winter forage species of most importance to northern caribou. Caribou excavate these through the snowpack using their broad hooves in a behaviour called cratering — consuming roughly 3 kg of lichen per day. Snow depth significantly affects access to this critical food: once snow exceeds approximately 50 centimetres, even high-lichen areas become inaccessible.

The FCH’s core winter range east of Ross River benefits from a pronounced snow-shadow effect created by the Pelly Mountains intercepting moisture-laden weather systems from the southwest, keeping snow shallow and making lichen accessible throughout the winter. This is precisely why snow-depth anomalies matter: in 2007, deeper-than-normal snow near Caribou Lakes pushed the majority of the herd east toward the shallower snowpack around Finlayson Lake — an unusual distribution not seen in any previous survey.

Lichen characteristics and recovery time

Winter lichen availability is critical to evaluating caribou habitat. Highest densities are associated with mature forests, as lichen is very slow growing. Growth rates of reindeer lichen groups (Cladonia spp.) are approximately 5 mm per year, and the long recovery time for lichen forage has been suggested as a limiting factor for caribou populations. This slow recovery makes lichen mats highly vulnerable to disturbance — whether from fire, industrial footprint, or overgrazing — and explains why old-growth and mature forest stands are disproportionately important.

Northern mountain caribou rely on old growth forests because they have abundant amounts of terrestrial and arboreal lichen, and shallow snow depth due to interception by the canopy. Windswept areas also have shallow snow due to wind, which allows access to terrestrial lichen. Winter habitat is often in areas where a rain or snow shadow is present, which results in reduced precipitation.

Flexibility between lichen types

In winter, caribou forage on terrestrial lichens in both lowland lodgepole pine flats and on windswept alpine slopes, and on arboreal lichens in upper elevation Engelmann spruce and subalpine fir forests. In a year of record heavy snowfall, all collared caribou wintered on windswept alpine slopes, while during the below-average snowfall year, many remained in forested habitats. In late winter, when increased snow depths make cratering for terrestrial lichens difficult, caribou feed increasingly on arboreal lichens.

6.3 Predator avoidance as a habitat driver

For Northern Mountain caribou, habitat selection is not solely about forage — predator avoidance is an equally powerful force shaping where and when caribou use the landscape. This is especially important for understanding the FCH, whose primary limiting factor has been wolf predation rather than food availability.

Spatial separation from moose and wolves

The core mechanism is apparent competition: wolves primarily hunt moose, but will take caribou opportunistically. Where moose are abundant, wolf numbers are sustained at levels that inflict significant mortality on caribou. Caribou avoid predation primarily by spatially segregating from other ungulate species that are the primary prey of their shared predators. Selection of fen/bog complexes by caribou and selection of well-drained habitats by moose and wolves results in spatial separation, which in turn reduces wolf predation pressure on caribou — but does not provide a total refuge from wolves.

For mountain herds like the FCH, the equivalent mechanism is elevational separation: caribou use high alpine and subalpine habitat during calving and summer — terrain that wolves travel through less efficiently and where moose are largely absent. During calving, females are dispersed from moose and other wolf prey by selecting high elevation subalpine and parkland habitats.

Response to industrial linear features

All large mammal predator and prey species move faster on linear features (roads, cutlines). Wolves and bears are attracted towards linear features, whereas prey species avoid them. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that linear features facilitate predator movement and increase hunting efficiency, while prey perceive such features as risky.

This has direct relevance to the KZK mine Access Road: while caribou avoidance beyond 250 m is not statistically significant (Dyer et al. 2001), the road may still elevate effective predation risk by enabling faster wolf travel through the FCH’s rut and early winter range.

Disturbance avoidance and range fidelity

In summer, caribou avoided mines by 2 km and cabins and camps by 1.5 km, while in winter, when human activity was low, avoidance of these features was minor. This seasonal asymmetry is relevant to the FCH: the KZK mine would be most active during seasons when caribou are present for rutting and post-calving — the periods of highest avoidance response.

General caribou-habitat relationships — selection of conifer-dominated forests and treed lowlands, and avoidance of disturbed, settled and open areas and roads — are consistent with current management guidance. Fidelity is strongest for calving sites and home ranges used during the calving and post-calving seasons.

6.4 Key habitat threats and climate context

Threat Mechanism Relevance to FCH
Industrial development (roads, mines) Habitat avoidance, facilitated predator movement, direct mortality KZK mine in rut/post-calving range; Robert Campbell Hwy through winter range
Wildfire Destroys terrestrial lichen mats; recovery takes decades Lichen-rich lowland winter range vulnerable; fire exclusion historically maintained range quality
Snow depth anomalies Deep snow prevents cratering; shifts distribution Documented shift in 2007; Pelly Mountain snow shadow critical buffer
Lichen overgrazing Slow growth rate means mats can degrade under sustained grazing pressure Less acute for FCH given large range, but relevant if herd grows significantly
Shrub encroachment (climate warming) Reduces lichen cover in tundra and open forest; benefits moose which supports more wolves Emerging threat; satellite data show lichen cover declined in 62% of a large eastern Canadian study region since the 1980s, with warming-induced shrub encroachment a likely contributor
Predator-prey dynamics Wolf numbers track moose, not caribou; caribou suffer collateral predation Dominant limiting factor for FCH historically; wolf control 1983–1989 demonstrated dramatic response

Why habitat is not the current limiting factor for the FCH

The EDI review (2019) and the population reports both emphasise that, unlike many boreal caribou populations in southern Canada where habitat loss from logging is the primary driver of decline, the FCH’s trajectory has been governed principally by mortality — specifically wolf predation and harvest. The Pelly Mountains winter range remains largely intact old-growth lichen forest with low industrial footprint, and the snow-shadow microclimate has not substantially changed. This distinction is important for management: actions targeting predation and harvest regulation (as implemented 2018–2019) are expected to be more effective than habitat protection alone at stabilising this particular herd.
