Technical Review and Evolutionary Analysis: Finlayson Caribou Herd (FCH) Assessment for the Kudz Ze Kayah Project

2.1 Strategic Context and Assessment Framework for the FCH

The Finlayson Caribou Herd (FCH) is not merely a biological population; it is a Valued Environmental and Socio-economic Component (VESEC) that serves as the nexus for ecological integrity and the Kaska Way of Life. The 2020 Yukon Environmental and Socio-economic Assessment Board (YESAB) screening represents a landmark in the territory’s regulatory history, as the Executive Committee was forced to reconcile a 38-year industrial lifecycle with the long-term viability of a sensitive woodland caribou population. Under the Yukon Environmental and Socio-economic Assessment Act (YESAA) and the Species at Risk Act (SARA), the assessment framework transitioned from standard project-level mitigation to a rigorous evaluation of the herd’s role in cultural and ecological stability.

By utilizing the Valued Component methodology, the Executive Committee elevated the FCH from a set of isolated biological metrics to a cornerstone of jurisdictional policy. This approach acknowledges that for the Liard First Nation (LFN) and Ross River Dena Council (RRDC), caribou conservation is inseparable from Traditional Land Use and the exercise of Aboriginal rights. The assessment was framed not just by the project footprint, but by the legal necessity to protect the “special relationship between Yukon Indian persons and the wilderness environment.” The 2020 determination was ultimately driven by baseline findings that revealed a herd characterized by significant vulnerability, necessitating a shift toward landscape-level oversight.

2.2 Summary and Evaluation of Section 6.1 Findings (2020)

The 2020 baseline findings established that the FCH was in a precarious state long before the proposed commencement of the Kudz Ze Kayah (KZK) Project. A technical understanding of these population trends and spatial requirements was critical to justifying the “Significant Adverse Effect” determination.

Population Metrics and Recruitment Trends

A primary indicator of the herd’s health is the ratio of calves per 100 cows during fall rut surveys, which serves as a proxy for recruitment. Analysis of the data in Figure 17 reveals a critical historical trend: recruitment was significantly higher during the wolf control period (1983-1991). Following the cessation of active predator management, recruitment showed high variability and a general downward pressure. This data highlights the precarious nature of the FCH; the herd lacks the surplus resiliency to absorb additional stressors, making any industrial impact on adult survival or calf recruitment a threat to the population’s self-sustaining status.

Habitat Mapping and the Zone of Influence (ZOI)

Spatial modeling identified that the project directly intersects with high-quality habitat essential for the caribou life cycle:

  • Seasonal Distribution: The project footprint sits on high-quality suitable habitat used during the Rut and Post-calving periods (Figures 14 and 15), which are the most sensitive phases for population replacement.
  • Game Management Subzones (GMS): The mine is situated within GMS 10-07, an area that serves as a vital corridor for winter and post-rutting activity.
  • The 3 km Buffer: Modeling identifies a Zone of Influence (ZOI) extending 3 km from the project boundary (Figure 20). Within this ZOI, sensory disturbances-noise, light, and traffic-are predicted to alter caribou behavior, leading to effective habitat loss and increased energetic costs of displacement.

Significance Determination: The 38-Year Risk

The Executive Committee determined that the project would result in Significant Adverse Effects despite the Proponent’s initial mitigation designs. From a policy perspective, the permanence of the risk is the deciding factor. With a project lifespan of 38 years (construction through post-closure), the duration of displacement exceeds the reproductive lifespan of several generations of caribou. This long-term exclusion from high-quality habitat in GMS 10-07 constitutes a significant threat that standard site-level mitigations cannot resolve. Because the project footprint sits directly on critical Rut and Post-calving habitat, the Committee determined that only the power to seasonally suspend operations could potentially reduce these risks to an acceptable level.

2.3 Critical Analysis of Mandated Mitigations and Oversight

The 2020 Recommendation fundamentally altered the regulatory landscape by moving from “siloed project mitigation” to landscape-level management. This shift moved the primary burden of caribou protection from a Proponent-led model to a government-led co-management framework.

The Oversight Body (Mitigation #10)

The creation of an Oversight Body is a sophisticated regulatory tool designed to manage the energetic costs of displacement in real-time. This body, comprised of the Proponent, Government of Yukon, LFN, and RRDC, possesses the authority to override operational schedules based on caribou presence.

  • Power to Suspend: The body can mandate the temporary suspension of blasting, crushing, and truck transportation based on seasonal sensitivity.
  • Sensory Disturbance: Prioritization of methods to reduce noise and light within the ZOI.
  • Aviation Control: Authority to modify flight paths or enforce a suspension of helicopter use.
  • Adaptive Triggers: Identification of biological “triggers” that necessitate immediate corrective action and the identification of mitigations based on “New Knowledge” (Mitigation 10.a.v).

FCH Range Management Plan (Mitigation #11)

A significant policy shift occurs in Mitigation #11, which mandates the Government of Yukon to develop a comprehensive plan. This forces a cumulative-effects management style onto the territorial government, ensuring the KZK project is managed within the context of the entire herd range.

Component Policy Requirement and Scope
a. Management Principles Must be informed by Traditional Knowledge and the Kaska relationship with caribou.
b. Current Conditions Identify the health, trend, and condition of the population and habitat using best available info.
c. Limiting Factors Identify factors including habitat, predation, climate change, and human activities (harvest/land-use).
d. Future Conditions Scenarios for future impacts from development, wildfire, and climate.
e. Objectives & Thresholds Specific population/habitat objectives and thresholds of change that cannot be exceeded.
f. Management Tools Identify voluntary and non-voluntary tools, linked to regulatory and land-use planning.
g. Implementation Detailed plan for collaborative and timely execution of management actions.
h. Monitoring Identify ongoing needs, uncertainties, and time-bound learning plans to address data gaps.
i. Adaptive Management Mechanism for incorporating monitoring results and “new knowledge” into plan revisions.
j. Communication Establishing methods for sharing results and guidance with communities and decision-makers.

Analytical Insight: By tethering the project’s success to this Range Management Plan, the Yukon Government has effectively ended the era of “site-only” mining assessments. Long-term accountability for the FCH now rests on a collaborative, landscape-wide effort where the Proponent’s social license is contingent upon the broader health of the herd.

2.4 Post-2020 Literature Review: The Evolving Landscape of FCH Conservation

As per Mitigation 10.a.v, the “New Knowledge” trigger is the primary mechanism for the project to adapt to shifting environmental realities. Since the 2020 screening, the intersection of climate change and industrial disturbance has become even more acute.

Climate-Induced Habitat Shifts and Trigger Application

The 2020 projections-specifically the 2 degree Celsius temperature rise and the trend of snowfall arriving later (Section 5.2)-are no longer distant threats but active management concerns.

  • Energetic Costs: Warmer, more variable winters lead to increased icing events and deeper, heavier snow. This restricts access to ground lichen and increases the energetic costs of displacement for caribou forced to move away from the project ZOI.
  • Trigger Mechanism: The “New Knowledge” trigger must now account for the fact that the FCH may be forced to utilize the project area more intensely if surrounding ranges are lost to wildfire or if traditional wintering grounds become energetically too costly to access due to snow conditions.

Indigenous-Led Conservation and Modern Standards

Since 2020, the “Kaska Way of Life” has evolved into a more formal framework for Indigenous-led conservation. Collaborative co-management models have moved beyond simple consultation to active, on-the-ground stewardship. Modern caribou conservation standards now emphasize that sensory disturbance modeling must be coupled with Indigenous observations of herd behavior.

Conclusion: The Necessity of Adaptive Management

The 2020 “Significance Determination” remains robust precisely because it anticipated these shifts. The mitigations provided a “learning loop” via the Oversight Body and the Range Management Plan. However, as climate change accelerates the degradation of caribou habitat, the thresholds of change defined in Mitigation #11 become even more critical. In the current landscape, adaptive management is not an optional administrative process; it is a vital survival mechanism for the Finlayson Caribou Herd and a non-negotiable requirement for the project’s ongoing regulatory compliance.
